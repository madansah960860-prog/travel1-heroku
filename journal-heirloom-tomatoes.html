Why the ugly tomato costs us more

By Marisol Vandergriff, executive chef July 14, 2026 9 minute read

Every August somebody at a table starts the same conversation with me. Why does the tomato plate run sixteen dollars in a week when the supermarket is selling tomatoes at three dollars a pound? It is a reasonable thing to ask, and it earns a proper answer instead of the word "local" repeated twice and a smile.

Short answer: we buy fruit that the supermarket is not allowed to sell at full price, and we pay over the odds to get it. It sounds like nonsense. It is not, and here is the reasoning.

Grading was built to solve a shipping problem

The United States Department of Agriculture puts out voluntary grade standards for fresh tomatoes. They deal in shape, evenness of colour, absence of scars, cracks, catfacing and sunscald, and a size range taken across the widest point. Hit the top grade and your fruit is round, unmarked, firm and consistent. Not one line in those standards concerns flavour, for the simple reason that nobody can measure flavour with a caliper on a loading dock at four in the morning.

None of this is a plot. The standards were written for a real reason. Send a truckload of tomatoes fifteen hundred miles and you need fruit that survives the journey, stacks without bruising, and turns up looking exactly like the photograph on the shelf tag. When produce has to travel, firmness and uniformity are precisely the right targets. The trouble is that breeding hard for firmness quietly breeds away from taste. Thicker cell walls give you a tomato that ships beautifully and eats like a wet tennis ball.

So a grading system turned into a breeding brief without anyone deciding it should. Wholesale growers are paid on grade, which means they plant what grades. Stretch that across a few decades and the commercial tomato drifts toward round, red, firm, even, and almost entirely without character.

What falls out the bottom

Now follow the fruit that fails. A healed crack across the shoulder. A Brandywine that came out lobed and creased. A Cherokee Purple with a green shoulder that will never redden because it was never meant to. Something that measured four inches when the pack called for three. All off-grade. It goes for processing at a sliver of the price, or it stays where it grew.

The ugly arithmetic: a small farm growing varieties that genuinely taste of something will lose a serious share of its crop on looks alone. Across the six farms inside our radius that take tomatoes seriously, off-grade lands somewhere between a quarter and a third of everything picked in a decent year. Give them a wet August and it can climb past forty percent.

That fruit is not lost because there is anything wrong with it. It is lost for being the wrong shape.

Our actual agreement

Since 2017 our tomato contracts have run on three rules. The price per pound is agreed before anything goes in the ground, not at harvest. The volume is committed for a whole season rather than negotiated week to week. And we buy on flavour and ripeness instead of grade, which in practice means taking a great deal of fruit a wholesaler would turn away at the gate.

What we pay sits roughly twenty percent above the wholesale rate for graded fruit. That is the figure people snag on. We hand over more for produce the conventional market has priced lower.

The logic is not complicated. Promise a grower that the cracked and lobed fruit already has somewhere to go and you change what they decide to plant. Suddenly the varieties worth eating beat the varieties built to survive a pallet. Hominy Creek Farm moved from four varieties to eleven across three seasons off the back of that agreement, and eight of those eleven would never clear a wholesale grade.

The twenty percent is not really buying tomatoes. It is buying a say in what goes into the ground.

What it means in the kitchen

Buying like this drags the cooking somewhere else, and that is the part I genuinely look forward to.

  • Fruit arrives at wildly different ripeness on the same day, so we sort at the door into three buckets: slice today, cook tomorrow, sauce by Thursday.
  • A cracked tomato has a short life. Anything with a break in the skin goes into service that day or into the pot that afternoon.
  • We cannot promise a specific variety on a specific night, which is why the menu says heirloom tomato and the server tells you which four are on the plate.
  • Nothing is refrigerated. Cold destroys the volatile compounds that make a good tomato smell like a tomato, and once they are gone they do not come back.

The toast is the plainest thing on our card and the one you cannot fake. Levain, cultured butter, sliced fruit, salt, torn basil. Give it a mediocre tomato and there is nowhere for the dish to hide. That exposure is the entire reason it stays on the menu.

The honest downside

I will not pretend any of this comes without cost or risk.

  1. We waste more than a kitchen buying graded fruit. Soft and split tomatoes do not last, and even with a same-day plan we lose a few percent.
  2. The plate price has to carry the higher cost per pound, which is why it is sixteen dollars and not nine.
  3. When a season goes badly, it goes badly for us specifically. In 2019 a late blight took most of two farms in ten days and we had no tomato dish for three weeks. We wrote that on the chalkboard rather than buying in from Florida.
  4. It takes more labour. Sorting a mixed crate takes a cook fifteen minutes that a uniform case would not.

We take all four on, because the alternative is a tomato plate indistinguishable from every other tomato plate. The world has plenty of those already and none of them need our help.

So when the toast reaches you this month and one slice is deep purple with a green shoulder while the next is orange and faintly lopsided, nothing has gone wrong in the kitchen. That is the argument itself, served on bread.

Marisol Vandergriff

Marisol runs the kitchens in all three Embervine rooms and still writes every menu out by hand before it reaches type. She was raised in Madison County, North Carolina, and cooked in Charleston and Chicago before coming home in 2013.
