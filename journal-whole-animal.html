Taking the whole animal, and what that forces onto the menu

By Desmond Achebe, co-owner and operations June 2, 2026 8 minute read

In spring 2015 a hog farmer up in Marshall offered a better price if we would take whole animals rather than cases of pork chops. We agreed, and we agreed for the wrong reason: it was cheaper. A decade on, no other decision has shaped this company as much, and the money saved has turned out to be the least interesting thing about it.

The arithmetic nobody explains

A finished hog of about 250 pounds live weight hangs at roughly 180 pounds of carcass, and boning that out leaves somewhere near 140 pounds of usable meat and fat. Break it down properly, counting trim, fat, bones and offal as their own items, and you end up with something like forty-two separate cuts and products.

And here is the catch. Six of those cuts move on their own name: loin chops, tenderloin, belly, shoulder for pulling, ribs, and the ham up to a point. A restaurant buying cases buys those six and nothing else, because those are the ones guests order without being talked into anything.

Buy the whole animal and those six arrive alongside everything else, in exactly the ratio the pig grew them. One hog yields maybe eight to ten loin chops. It also hands you two full shoulders, forty pounds of fat, a set of trotters, the jowls, the heart, the liver, the kidneys, the caul fat, the skin and a bucket of trim.

No hog was ever sold on its chops. There are nowhere near enough of them. That is the whole lesson in one line.

What it forces onto a menu

The moment the animal is in your walk-in, the menu stops being a wish list and turns into a plan for using what is already hanging there. That constraint has produced more good dishes in this kitchen than any brainstorm ever managed.

  • Jowl becomes guanciale, cured for three weeks and then used in almost everything that needs a savoury backbone.
  • Fat gets rendered into lard, which is why our pastry shell tastes the way it does and why the duck fat potatoes have a second fat in the pan.
  • Trim becomes sausage, which becomes the sausage in the beans, which becomes a staff meal on Sunday.
  • Bones become stock, which becomes the base of the braise, which is the lamb shoulder dish on the current menu.
  • Skin gets scored, dried overnight and fried into a bar snack that outsells three cocktails.
  • Heart is trimmed, sliced thin, grilled hard over the coals and served with sorghum and chili. It is the best value dish in the building and about half the guests who order it have never eaten heart before.

None of it shows up on a menu written out of a distributor catalogue. It shows up when you are staring at forty pounds of fat and a problem.

The real costs

Let me be specific here, because whole-animal buying attracts a lot of romance and the romance never includes the invoice.

  1. Labour. Breaking a hog properly takes a trained butcher between ninety minutes and two hours. At our wage that is roughly forty dollars of labour per animal, every week, forever. A case of chops takes zero minutes.
  2. Storage. An animal takes up walk-in space in a way that cases do not, and it takes it up all at once. We had to add a second cabinet in 2016 purely to handle carcasses and dry-ageing at the same time.
  3. Skill dependency. If your butcher leaves, you have a serious problem. We now cross-train every line cook on breakdown for exactly this reason, which costs more training hours.
  4. Menu inflexibility. If the heart dish does not sell this week, we still have hearts next week. You cannot simply stop ordering the parts that move slowly.

Set against that, the savings are genuine but slimmer than people imagine. We pay about $4.10 per pound hanging weight, which lands near $5.30 per pound of usable meat once bone and loss are accounted for. The same weight bought as individual primal cuts would sit closer to $7.40. The raw gap matters, and then labour claws a good piece of it straight back.

Where the money actually comes from

The margin was never in the discount. It lives in what you do with the cheap parts.

At thirty-nine dollars, the pork chop carries a food cost of about thirty percent. That is an ordinary, respectable figure and it is what most restaurants run on. The braised shoulder, cut from something that costs us a fraction of the loin, comes in nearer nineteen percent. The sausage in the bean dish sits under fifteen. Stock built from bones we already own costs roughly what onions and time cost.

So the six cuts that sell themselves cover the rent, and the thirty-six that do not are where the profit actually sits. That inversion never comes up when people discuss nose-to-tail cooking as an ethical stance. It is an ethical stance. It is also the reason this company came through 2020.

What we throw away

Almost nothing, and there is no pride in saying so, because reaching that point took years. Waste off a broken hog now runs under three percent by weight, nearly all of it connective tissue that refuses to render plus a little bone dust.

Nothing clever got us to that number. It was a run of unglamorous decisions: buy a grinder, buy a sausage stuffer, build a curing cabinet, teach everyone to make stock, and make peace with a walk-in that permanently holds a tray of something due by Thursday.

Eat here often enough and you have worked through most of an animal without ever really noticing. That is the point. A dish has to be good first and virtuous second, and if the only argument for ordering something is that it stops waste, the kitchen has not cooked it well enough yet.

Desmond Achebe

Desmond looks after leases, payroll, purchasing and the produce truck across all three rooms. He spent six years on the line before shifting to operations, which he reckons is the only reason anybody in the kitchen listens to a word he says.
