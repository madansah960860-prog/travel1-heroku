Eleven years feeding the same starter

By Rebecca Tull, head baker May 9, 2026 9 minute read

The culture behind every loaf in all three of our rooms began life in a plastic quart container in October 2016, built from stone-ground rye flour and tap water on Biltmore Avenue. It has taken two feeds a day, every day, from that morning to this one. Never dried, never deliberately frozen, never swapped out.

People want to know whether any of that matters or whether it is just a line we like repeating. Honestly, it matters less than bakers insist and more than the sceptics allow, and the interesting part sits in the detail.

What a starter actually is

A starter is a settled community of wild yeasts and lactic acid bacteria living together in a paste of flour and water. The yeasts throw off carbon dioxide, and that is what lifts the loaf. The bacteria throw off lactic and acetic acid, and that is what supplies the flavour, the keeping quality and the faintly tacky crumb.

That community settles into a balance shaped entirely by how you feed it. Feed often and warm and the lactic bacteria take over, which reads as a milder, yoghurt-like sourness. Feed sparingly and cool and the acetic side climbs, which reads sharper and more vinegary. Change the flour and the whole equilibrium moves, because you have changed what it eats.

So an eleven-year-old starter is not eleven years of stockpiled magic. It is eleven years of unchanging conditions producing a culture tuned very precisely to our flour, our room temperature and our schedule. That tuning is the actual asset. A fresh starter would need roughly three months to become as predictable, and predictability is the only reason I can put out 180 loaves a day without thinking hard about any single one of them.

The schedule

The working culture sits at about 78 degrees Fahrenheit in a covered tub beside the proofing cabinet. Feeds land at 4:00 AM and 4:00 PM on a one to three to three ratio by weight: one part starter, three parts water, three parts flour.

The flour is a blend we landed on in 2019 and have left alone ever since:

  • Seventy percent white bread flour from a mill in Cleveland County, milled from soft red winter wheat.
  • Twenty percent whole rye, which is what the culture was started on and what it is happiest eating.
  • Ten percent whole wheat, stone ground, for the extra mineral content that keeps fermentation lively.

A second culture sits in a jar in the walk-in, fed once a week and kept purely as insurance. A third has been dried to flakes and sealed into two mason jars, one of which lives in my own kitchen at home. The reason for that comes in a moment.

How it changes across a year

This is not the same organism in January that it is in July, and any baker claiming a process fixed year round has stopped paying attention.

  1. Summer. The kitchen sits warm and the culture accelerates. Bulk fermentation drops from around five hours to three and a half. We shorten the final proof and bake earlier, and the bread runs slightly milder because the lactic side takes over.
  2. Autumn. The most stable period of the year. New crop flour comes in around October with higher enzyme activity, which makes the dough slacker for a few weeks until we adjust the hydration down by two percent.
  3. Winter. Everything slows. Bulk stretches to six or seven hours, so the overnight retard gets shorter to compensate. The bread is noticeably sharper in February than in June, and regulars notice.
  4. Spring. The hardest season to read, because the room temperature swings ten degrees between a cold morning and a warm afternoon. This is when we rely most on watching the dough rather than the clock.

The week in 2021

In March 2021 the compressor on the main walk-in gave out on a Saturday night and nobody caught it until Sunday morning. The box had drifted up to 61 degrees. Almost everything inside was fine, or close enough to rescue. The backup starter jar, fed four days earlier and long past its peak, was neither.

I opened it and the smell was wrong straight away. Sharp in a way that read as acetone rather than vinegar, a grey liquid layer floating on top, and a pink cast at the rim where the paste met the glass. Pink or orange discolouration is the single sign that means bin it rather than try to save it, because it can point to bacterial contamination that has nothing to do with a healthy culture.

The working culture in the bakery came through untouched, because it sits at room temperature and had taken its 4:00 AM feed as usual. We lost the backup and nothing besides. It remains the closest call we have had, and it changed how we work for good.

Since then there are always three: the working culture, a fed backup in a separate refrigeration unit, and dried flakes held off site. Drying takes nothing much. Spread the starter thin across parchment, leave it at room temperature for two days until it turns brittle, break it into flakes and seal it up. It will come back years later.

What it gives the bread

A well-tuned culture buys you three things: a rise you can count on, a crumb that stays soft for two days instead of one, and a flavour with real structure behind it rather than one flat sour note.

The loaf reaches service as the levain and butter plate, turns into the base of the sungold tomato toast through summer, gets torn under the clay pot chicken, and shows up as seeded crisps alongside the trout rillettes whenever we bake the rye version on Thursdays. One culture, five jobs.

The starter is not a mascot. It is a piece of equipment that happens to be alive, and it asks for the same discipline as everything else in a kitchen: the same feed, at the same hour, every day, whether or not there is anyone around to see it.

Rebecca Tull

Rebecca has run the bakery since 2018 and owns that starter in every sense that counts. She sets the flour blend, bakes for all three rooms out of the Asheville kitchen, and clocks on at 3:40 in the morning six days a week.
